# Veda Business Service Analytics

## Overview

Build a complete data science analytics solution for Veda Technology by analyzing simulated business data related to website visitors, internship applications, training programs, IT services, digital services, inquiries, customer/service requests, marketing channels, and business performance.

**Objective:** Give interns practical exposure to how data science can support a growing technology and service organization through business intelligence, customer analysis, service analytics, marketing analysis, forecasting, and data-driven decision making.

Run the notebooks in listed part order. Later parts may rely on datasets generated by earlier parts.

## Parts

## Part 1 - Day 35: Veda Technology Business & Service Analytics

Since the project forbids private data, I generated a synthetic dataset of seven tables for one year (October 2025 to September 2026) with a fixed random seed: `website_visits` (24,000 visits), `internship_applications` (2,600), `training_enrollments`, `it_service_inquiries`, `digital_service_inquiries`, `service_requests`, and `marketing_campaigns`. The tables follow simple rules I chose (a growth trend, busier weekdays, seasonal peaks, and relationships such as bounce depending on channel and device, or selection depending on the assessment score). I then made each table deliberately messy (duplicate rows, missing values, two date formats, inconsistent labels like `FB` and `Google Ads`, and impossible numbers such as negative session durations) and saved them as raw CSV files in `datasets/veda_business/raw/`. For the data cleaning and preprocessing pipeline, I wrote one function, `clean_table()`, driven by a short specification for each table, that removes duplicates, parses both date formats, standardizes labels, sets impossible numbers to missing, fills gaps with group medians (or `Unknown` for labels), and converts whole-number columns back to integers. It turned 34,269 raw rows into 34,060 clean rows, removing 209 duplicates, standardizing 5,041 labels, and handling 195 impossible numbers. Two gaps were left on purpose, survey non-responses and the resolution time of requests that are still open, because the gap itself carries meaning. I checked the result with validation checks and by comparing the cleaned tables against the original clean ones, which only a simulation allows. For the website visitor analysis, I calculated KPIs (visits, bounce rate, pages per visit, session duration, conversion rate) and broke traffic down by month, weekday, channel, device, landing page, region, and conversion type. For the internship application analysis, I looked at applications by month and domain, selection rates by college tier, education level, and program length, the link between assessment score and outcome, and the selection and enrollment rate of each application source, and I repeated the domain analysis in SQL with `sqlite3` to confirm it matches pandas. The cleaned tables are saved in `datasets/veda_business/cleaned/` for the next days of the project.

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, I had a reproducible simulated business dataset, a reusable cleaning pipeline that recovered every original record, and the first two analyses of the project. The website received 24,000 visits with a 47.7% bounce rate and a 3.64% conversion rate. Email (8.38%) and Referral (6.35%) converted best, while Social Media (1.71%) and Paid Ads (2.05%) converted worst even though Social Media supplied 24% of the traffic. Mobile visitors, who make up 63% of visits, convert at less than half the desktop rate. For internships, 36.1% of the 2,600 applicants were selected, selection followed the assessment score and the college tier (Tier 1 at 50% versus Tier 3 at 31%), and Referral and Campus Outreach applicants enrolled most often (95% and 92% of those selected). Because the data is simulated, these findings reflect the assumptions I built into it, so they practice the analysis workflow and do not describe the real company. The main lesson was how much of the work is making messy data trustworthy before any analysis, and how a specification-driven pipeline handles seven tables with one function.

## Part 2 - Day 36: Veda Technology Business & Service Analytics

I continued from the cleaned tables saved on Day 35 in `datasets/veda_business/cleaned/`, loading `training_enrollments`, `it_service_inquiries`, `digital_service_inquiries`, and `service_requests` with `parse_dates`. A few gaps were left in those tables on purpose (survey non-responses and the resolution time of requests that are still open), so I excluded open requests from every resolution-time figure instead of filling them. For the training program performance analysis, I calculated KPIs (enrollments, fee revenue, completion rate, attendance, final score, satisfaction), compared the six programs on enrollments, revenue share, completion, and satisfaction, and then looked at delivery mode, lead source, completion rate by attendance band, the attendance-score and score-satisfaction correlations, and enrollments by month. For the IT and digital service inquiry analyses, I wrote shared helper functions (`prepare_inquiries()`, `inquiry_kpis()`, `inquiry_summary()`, and `plot_inquiries()`) so that both tables are analyzed in exactly the same way: an inquiry counts as converted when its status is `Converted`, and the response time is grouped into three bands (within 4 hours, 4 to 24 hours, over 24 hours) with `pd.cut()` to test whether speed affects conversion. I then compared conversion, average budget, and deal value by service, client type, and channel, and put the IT and digital KPIs side by side. For the customer and service request analysis, I calculated the resolved, escalated, and open shares, compared the service areas and request types, and checked resolution time and satisfaction by priority and status. I also added a service-level check with assumed targets (Critical within 8 hours, High within 24, Medium within 48, Low within 72), since the data has no official targets, and I repeated the resolution time by priority in SQL with `sqlite3` to confirm it matches pandas. Monthly volume and escalation charts finish the analysis.

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, I had analyzed four more business areas of the project with the same workflow. The 1,800 training enrollments brought in INR 24.1 million, led by Full Stack Web Development (29.2% of revenue). The completion rate was 65.8% and was similar across programs, but higher for offline students (72.9% versus 63.7% online), and no student with under 60% attendance completed. IT and digital inquiries converted at 45.7% and 43.1%, in both cases faster responses converted better and Referral was the best channel, and IT deals were worth about 5.8 times the total value of digital deals, with Enterprise clients giving 39% of IT deal value from 13% of the inquiries. Of the 3,200 service requests, 84.6% were resolved, escalated requests left customers far less satisfied (2.83 versus 4.02), Billing Queries were the slowest to resolve, and Critical requests had the lowest compliance with the assumed targets (91.8%). Because the data is simulated, these findings reflect the assumptions I built into it, so they practice the analysis workflow and do not describe the real company. The main lesson was that volume alone can mislead: the busiest channel, service, or client group is often not the one that converts best or brings in the most money.

## Part 3 - Day 37: Veda Technology Business & Service Analytics

I continued from the seven cleaned tables saved on Day 35. For the marketing-channel performance analysis, I calculated the click-through rate, cost per click, lead rate, and cost per lead for each of the five channels with a marketing cost, compared each channel's share of spend with its share of leads, looked at monthly spend, leads, and cost per lead, and linked the spend to the website by calculating the cost per website visit and per website conversion. For the customer segmentation, I used two approaches. First, I clustered website visitors with `KMeans` on the pages viewed and the log of the session duration (both standardized with `StandardScaler`), compared silhouette scores for 2 to 6 segments on a sample of 5,000 visits, and chose 3 segments even though 2 scored highest, because the 2-segment solution agreed with the existing `bounced` flag for 99.3% of visits and added nothing new. I named the segments Quick leavers, Focused readers, and Deep explorers from their profiles. Second, I split the IT and digital inquiries into Small, Medium, and Large budget bands with `pd.cut()` and compared their conversion rate, response time, and deal value. For the service demand analysis, I combined internship applications, training enrollments, and IT and digital inquiries into monthly demand by business line and a demand index, ranked every individual service by demand and by value, and compared the channels that each line's demand comes from. For the business KPI dashboard, I built a scorecard of 26 KPIs across six business areas, a static dashboard in Matplotlib (saved as `business_kpi_dashboard.png`), and six tables for Power BI (a date table, a channel table, a monthly summary, a channel performance table, the service demand table, and the KPI scorecard), with a consistency check that the exported totals match the cleaned tables. Because a Power BI file has to be built in Power BI Desktop, I wrote `power_bi_dashboard_guide.md` with the import steps, relationships, DAX measures, page layout, and the expected values to check the result against.

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, I had analyzed marketing, segmented customers in two ways, measured service demand, and prepared the business dashboard. Paid Ads and Social Media use 76% of the marketing budget for only 38% of the leads, while Referral and Email deliver 29% and 19% of the leads from 7% and 5% of the spend, at about INR 19 to 20 per lead. 48% of website visits are Quick leavers who produce 0.7% of conversions, and Large inquiries are only 12% of the inquiries but 52% of the deal value. Internships and training dominate demand by volume, but the five most valuable services are all IT services, and each business line draws on a different mix of channels. The dashboard tables reconcile exactly with the cleaned data. Because the data is simulated, these findings reflect the assumptions I built into it, so they practice the analysis workflow and do not describe the real company. The main lesson was that no single measure tells the whole story: volume, conversion, cost, and value pointed to different channels and services, so a dashboard has to show them together.

## Part 4 - Day 38: Veda Technology Business & Service Analytics

I continued from the cleaned tables saved on Day 35 and did two predictive analyses. For the demand forecast, I built the daily website visits series and ran a backtest, training on October to June and testing on the last 92 days (July to September), with two models: a weekday average of the last 28 training days and a `LinearRegression` on the day number and weekday dummies. Because one year of data cannot teach a model the yearly seasonal pattern, I then made a planning forecast for October to December 2026 as a seasonal naive forecast (the same months of last year) with three growth scenarios of 0%, 10%, and 20%. For the inquiry conversion model, I combined the IT and digital inquiries, trained only on inquiries with an outcome (`Converted` or `Lost`, since inquiries still in progress have no outcome yet), and used a stratified 80/20 split. The predictors were the service line, service type, client type, channel, the budget, and the response time (as a number and as flags for replies within 4 hours and after more than 24 hours), with the status and deal value deliberately excluded to avoid leakage. I compared a `LogisticRegression` and a `RandomForestClassifier` inside `ColumnTransformer` pipelines with a baseline and with a model that only knows the reply speed, used repeated 5-fold cross-validation because a single test set this size is noisy, and checked how well the model ranks inquiries with a lift table by predicted-probability group. I also scored the 604 inquiries still in progress. For the business recommendations, I turned the findings of the four days into eight recommendations, each with evidence, an action, and an illustrative impact calculated from the data (for example, a marketing budget shift, a 4-hour reply target, and a mobile conversion gap), and saved them as `business_recommendations.csv`. For the executive-level analytics report, I wrote `executive_report.md` and a PDF version, `executive_report.pdf`, with a summary, KPIs, key insights, the outlook, the recommendations, limitations, next steps, and the project documentation (repository structure and how to reproduce the results).

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, I had finished the project with a forecast, a tested prediction model, recommendations, and an executive report. The demand backtest showed that a trend and weekday model captures the weekly pattern (14% average daily error) but misses the July and August peak, so the Q4 2026 plan is a range of 1,602 to 1,922 applications, enrollments, and inquiries. The conversion model turned out to be weak (ROC AUC 0.57, cross-validated mean 0.58), no better than a model that only knows whether the reply was fast, and its lift table was irregular, so I concluded that it cannot rank individual inquiries, and that the useful finding is that conversion depends on reply speed and channel and not on the client or the service. The three high-priority recommendations are rebalancing the marketing budget (about 3,540 more leads a year in the scenario), answering inquiries within 4 hours (about 234 more deals), and improving the mobile experience (about 236 more conversions), all upper-end scenarios that should be tested before they are scaled. Because the data is simulated, these findings reflect the assumptions I built into it and practice the analysis workflow, but they do not describe the real company. The main lesson was that an honest negative result is still a result: reporting that the model was weak, and why, was more useful than presenting a model that only looked good.
