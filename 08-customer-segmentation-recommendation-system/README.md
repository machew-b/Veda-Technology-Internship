# Customer Segmentation Recommendation System

## Overview

Develop a customer analytics system that groups customers based on behavior, interests, engagement, service inquiries, spending, or interaction patterns and recommends suitable Veda Technology services or learning programs.

**Objective:** Learn unsupervised learning, customer segmentation, feature engineering, clustering evaluation, and recommendation logic.

Run the notebooks in listed part order. Later parts may rely on datasets generated by earlier parts.

## Parts

## Part 1 - Day 40: Customer Segmentation & Service Recommendation System

Since the project asks for synthetic or public data, I generated a synthetic customer dataset of 5,000 customers and 16 columns (sign-up date, customer type, region, acquisition channel, primary interest, website visits, session length, pages per visit, email open rate, days since last activity, service inquiries, support tickets, purchases, total spend, and a satisfaction rating) with a fixed random seed. Each customer is generated from one of four hidden behavior patterns (highly engaged learners, occasional visitors, new customers, and service-focused business clients), which is not saved in the data, so the segmentation on Day 41 has to find the groups from behavior alone. I then made the table deliberately messy (duplicate rows, missing values, two date formats, inconsistent labels like `SMB` and `Google Ads`, spending typed as text like `₹12,500`, impossible numbers such as negative session lengths, and contradictory values such as spending without purchases) and saved it as `datasets/veda_customers/raw/customers_raw.csv`. For the data preprocessing pipeline, I wrote one function, `clean_customers()`, driven by a short specification, that removes duplicates, parses both date formats, converts numbers typed as text, standardizes labels, sets impossible numbers to missing, applies logic rules (a customer cannot be inactive for longer than they have been a customer, and no purchases means no spending), fills gaps with the median of the customer type (or `Unknown` for labels), and converts whole-number columns back to integers. It turned 5,100 raw rows into 5,000 clean rows, removing 100 duplicates, converting 56 text numbers, standardizing 2,305 labels, and handling 114 impossible numbers and 25 contradictory values. The satisfaction survey gaps were left on purpose, because the gap itself means the customer did not answer. I checked the result with validation checks and by comparing the cleaned table with the original clean one, which only a simulation allows, and saved it as `datasets/veda_customers/cleaned/customers_cleaned.csv`. For the customer behavior analysis, I calculated key figures (active, inactive, and buying customers, spending, and survey response), looked at who the customers are by type, channel, region, interest, and sign-up month, and compared engagement (visits, session length, email opens, and days since last activity) and spending by customer type, channel, and interest. Because spending is very skewed, I used medians, a logarithmic axis, and a concentration curve, and I used the Spearman correlation for the relationships. I also looked at inquiries, support tickets, and satisfaction, and at scatter plots to see whether natural groups appear before any clustering, and I repeated the spending summary by customer type in SQL with `sqlite3` to confirm it matches pandas.

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, I had a reproducible simulated customer dataset, a preprocessing pipeline that recovered every original customer, and the behavior analysis that prepares Day 41. The 5,000 customers produced INR 206.3 million in total spending, 60% of customers had bought, and 69% were active in the last 30 days. The typical customer engages lightly (a median of 6 website visits and a 30% email open rate). Students are the most engaged type, business customers ask the most service inquiries, and customers who came through Email or Referral engage and buy the most. Spending is extremely concentrated: the top 10% of customers hold 74% of it, business customers and IT and Digital Services interests account for about 79% of it, and the median customer spent INR 5,900 against an average of INR 41,254. The engagement measures move together, but no pair of measures separates the customers into clear groups, so the segmentation needs several standardized measures (with spending log-transformed) at once. Because the data is simulated, these findings reflect the assumptions I built into it, so they practice the analysis workflow and do not describe the real customers of Veda Technology. The main lesson was that averages mislead with customer data: the average customer spent about seven times the median, so the segmentation has to deal with the skew and cannot rely on any single measure.

## Part 2 - Day 41: Customer Segmentation & Service Recommendation System

I continued from the cleaned customer table saved on Day 40 in `datasets/veda_customers/cleaned/`. For the feature engineering, I first checked why the raw columns cannot go into K-Means directly (different scales, skew, and overlapping measures), and then applied a `log(1 + x)` transform to website visits, recency, inquiries, tickets, and spending, which reduced the skew of spending from 10.81 to -0.27. I checked whether the counts should be divided by tenure to make rates and decided against it, because the counts barely grow with tenure (correlations of 0.05 to 0.14), so tenure became a feature of its own. Because the four engagement measures (visits, session length, pages per visit, and email open rate) are all related (0.53 to 0.60), I merged them into one engagement score, so engagement would not count four times against spending. The clustering uses six features (engagement score, recency, inquiries, tickets, spending, and tenure), and I left out purchases (0.94 correlated with spending), the average order value and the buyer flag, the satisfaction rating (45% missing), and the categories, which are used to describe the segments on Day 42. The features were standardized with `StandardScaler`, and the feature table is saved as `datasets/veda_customers/features/customer_features.csv`. For the K-Means clustering, I ran `KMeans` with 20 starts for 2 to 8 clusters and judged each solution with the elbow method, the silhouette score, the Davies-Bouldin and Calinski-Harabasz indexes, and two stability tests with the adjusted Rand index (ten different random starts, and twenty samples of 80% of the customers). I chose 4 clusters, because the elbow, the Davies-Bouldin index, and stability agree on it (the groups become unstable from 5 clusters), and its silhouette is practically tied with the best value, which belongs to 3 clusters. The final clusters are numbered from the largest to the smallest. For the cluster evaluation, I looked at the cluster centers in standardized and original units, drew a silhouette plot with the score of each cluster, checked how the clusters change when the feature choices change (without tenure, without the log transform, with the four engagement measures separately, and with all the raw columns), and compared the clusters with customer type, primary interest, and acquisition channel, which were not used for clustering. The clustered customers (with each customer's cluster and silhouette) and the evaluation by number of clusters are saved in `datasets/veda_customers/clustered/`.

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, I had an engineered feature table, four customer clusters, and an evaluation that shows both what works and the limits of the result. The clusters are an engaged group (1,517 customers, 30%, with 19.4 website visits on average and 95% having bought), an inactive group (1,369, 27%, last active 86 days ago on average), recent sign-ups (1,243, 25%, an average of 32 days since signing up), and a service-heavy group (871 customers, 17%, with 6.1 inquiries each on average, mostly business customers) that accounts for 73% of all spending. The solution is stable (an adjusted Rand index of 0.95 across random starts and 0.99 across samples) and agrees 0.68 to 0.88 with the clusters from other feature choices. The average silhouette is only 0.29, so the groups overlap and are not sharply separated, and the service-heavy cluster is the least well defined (12.7% of its customers are closer to another cluster). Because the data is simulated with four hidden behavior patterns, finding four clusters is partly built in, so these results show that the method works and not that real Veda Technology customers form four groups. The main lesson was that no single score picks the number of clusters: the silhouette preferred 3 by a hair, the Davies-Bouldin index and the elbow preferred 4, and stability ruled out 5 or more, so the choice had to combine them.

## Part 3 - Day 42: Customer Segmentation & Service Recommendation System

I continued from the clustered customers saved on Day 41 in `datasets/veda_customers/clustered/` and the standardized feature table in `datasets/veda_customers/features/`. For the customer segment profiles, I named the four clusters with a rule that reads each cluster's center instead of its number (the most inquiries gives the Service-Focused Clients, the shortest tenure the New Customers, the highest engagement the Engaged Learners, and the remaining one the Occasional Visitors, which I then checked has the lowest engagement and the longest inactivity). I profiled each segment by its size, buyers, spending (median, average, and share of the total), satisfaction, survey response, behavior (visits, session length, pages, email opens, inactivity, inquiries, tickets, and tenure), and make-up by customer type, interest, and channel, which were not used for the clustering, and saved the profiles as `datasets/veda_customers/segments/segment_profiles.csv`. For the 2D cluster visualization, I used `PCA` on the six standardized features, drew the customers with the segment centers (and a second picture colored by silhouette), saved it as `customer_segments_pca.png`, read what the two axes mean from their loadings, and measured how faithful the picture is (59.6% of the variance is kept, and the nearest 2D center gives the same segment for 89.0% of the customers). For the service recommendation logic, I built a catalog of the 24 offers of Veda Technology (internship domains, training programs, IT services, and digital services) from the business data of Days 35 to 38, and tested with chi-square whether the outcome rates (selection, completion, and conversion) differ between the offers of a family. They do not (p-values of 0.24, 0.92, 0.27, and 0.59), so the offers are ranked by demand and not by outcome. The recommender is a list of seven rules in a function `recommend()`: the primary family follows the customer's interest (or the most common interest of the segment when it is unknown), the cross-sell is the neighboring family, the offer is the most demanded one of the family, each segment has an action, service recovery comes first for customers with 3 or more tickets or a rating of 2 or less, Engaged Learners with a rating of 4 or more are invited to the referral program, and every recommendation gets a confidence level from the silhouette and whether the interest is known. I checked that all 5,000 customers get a complete recommendation, that the offer follows the interest, and how often each exception applies. The results are saved in `datasets/veda_customers/segments/` as `customers_segmented.csv`, `service_catalog.csv`, and `service_recommendations.csv`.

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, the four clusters from Day 41 became four named segments with profiles, a 2D map, and a rule-based service recommendation for every customer. The Engaged Learners (1,517 customers, 30%) are active students and professionals, 95% of whom have bought, and they hold 22.8% of the spending. The Occasional Visitors (1,369, 27%) were last active 86 days ago on average and hold 1.2% of the spending. The New Customers (1,243, 25%) signed up 32 days ago on average, and 41% have bought. The Service-Focused Clients (871, 17%) are mostly business customers with 6.1 inquiries each, and they hold 72.7% of the spending but are the least satisfied group together with the Occasional Visitors (3.6). The PCA picture keeps 59.6% of the variance and gives the same segment for 89.0% of the customers: the Occasional Visitors are the clearest group, while the Service-Focused Clients overlap the Engaged Learners. In the recommendations, 88% of the Service-Focused Clients are pointed to IT or digital services and 84% of the Engaged Learners to training or internships, service recovery applies to 457 customers (39.4% of the Service-Focused Clients), the referral invitation to 887 Engaged Learners, and the confidence is High for 63% of the customers and Low for 11%. Because the customers and offers are simulated and the data has no record of which offers customers accepted, the recommendations cannot be scored, and customers who share an interest get the same lead offer because the interest is recorded only at the level of the family. So the rules are a documented starting point to test in real campaigns. The main lesson was to check what the data can support before ranking: the offers looked different by outcome, but the differences were within chance, so ranking by demand was the honest choice.

## Part 4 - Day 43: Customer Segmentation & Service Recommendation System

I continued from the segmented customers and recommendations saved on Day 42 in `datasets/veda_customers/segments/`. For the customer segment dashboard, I built it in two forms. The static one is a picture, `customer_segment_dashboard.png`, with six headline numbers, the 2D customer map, the share of customers against the share of spending, a heatmap of what each segment looks like (in standard deviations from the average), the recommended offer family of each segment, and the campaign workload (customers per action). Each segment keeps the same color and marker shape in every panel, so color is never the only cue, the heatmaps use one blue hue for amounts and a blue-red scale with a gray middle for values above or below average, and the palette was checked for color-blind safety with a validator. The interactive one is a Streamlit app, `app.py`, with filters (segment, customer type, and acquisition channel), key numbers that update with the filters, and five tabs (overview, segment map, segment profiles, recommendations, and about), a customer lookup by ID, and a CSV download of the filtered recommendations. I tested the app by simulating the filters and the lookup with `streamlit.testing`, by starting it in a browser, and by checking that its numbers match the notebook. For the business recommendations, I calculated the evidence from the saved tables (for example, the spending held by Service-Focused Clients with many tickets or a low rating, the customers who have not bought yet, and the buying rate by acquisition channel), wrote a scenario with a stated assumption for each recommendation (such as 10% of the New Customers who have not bought making a first purchase at the median spending), ranked them by the value at stake and the strength of the evidence, and saved them as `business_recommendations.csv` with the evidence, the action, and the estimated impact. For the final report, I wrote `final_report.md` with a summary, the method, the four segments, the recommendation logic, the business recommendations, the limitations, next steps, and the project documentation (repository structure and how to reproduce and run the dashboard). I re-ran the notebook from a clean copy to confirm that the saved recommendations are identical, and recalculated the cited figures independently from the saved tables.

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, the project was complete: an interactive dashboard, a static dashboard picture, eight business recommendations, and a final report. The dashboard shows that the 5,000 simulated customers fall into four segments of very different value. The 871 Service-Focused Clients (17% of the customers) hold 72.7% of the INR 206.3 million in spending, and 343 of them, who hold INR 63.2 million (30.6% of all spending), have 3 or more support tickets or a rating of 2 or less, so service recovery for them is the first priority (losing 10% of that spending would cost about INR 6.3 million). The other high-priority actions are a learning path for the 1,517 Engaged Learners (about INR 1.4 million if 10% bought one more program) and a referral invitation to the 887 who rated Veda Technology 4 or 5 (about INR 0.8 million if 5% brought one customer). The medium-priority actions are a welcome series for the 738 New Customers who have not bought, a low-cost re-engagement email for the Occasional Visitors, a shift of marketing budget toward Referral and Email, and better survey and offer tracking. The impact figures are scenarios with stated assumptions and not forecasts. Because the data is simulated, the segments overlap (average silhouette 0.29), and the recommendations have never been tested on real customers, the first next step is a controlled campaign, such as sending the recommended offer to half of a segment and comparing the responses. The main lesson was that a segmentation is only useful when it ends in actions: the segments became lists of customers per campaign, and the honest limits had to travel with them.
