# Sales Service Demand Forecasting

## Overview

Build a forecasting system that predicts future demand for technology services, digital services, training programs, or other business categories using historical time-series data.

**Objective:** Introduce time-series analysis, trend detection, seasonality, forecasting, model evaluation, and business planning.

Run the notebooks in listed part order. Later parts may rely on datasets generated by earlier parts.

## Parts

## Part 1 - Day 45: Sales & Service Demand Forecasting System

Since the project asks for synthetic historical data, I generated a three-year daily history (October 1, 2023 to September 30, 2026, which is 1,096 days) of the demand for the four service lines of Veda Technology (Internship Programs, Training Programs, IT Services, and Digital Services), with a fixed random seed. The earlier business project had only one year of data, which is not enough to separate growth from seasonality, so I used three years. Each row holds the day, the service line, the demand in units, the revenue, and whether a marketing campaign was running, and the volume of the final year is set close to the Day 35 dataset. The simulation has a growth trend that differs by line, a yearly pattern with a different peak month for each line, a weekly pattern (weekends are lower, and IT Services drop the most), public holidays with lower demand, campaigns that lift demand by about 30%, day-to-day noise that is larger than a plain count would have, and a change in the last months (a lift for Digital Services from June 2026 and a slowdown for IT Services from July 2026) so that the forecasting days can show why historical trends do not always predict future demand. None of this is saved in the data, so the next days have to find it. I then made the data deliberately messy, like a real export (80 missing rows including five days with no data at all, an outage with six zero days, 12 spikes with an extra zero, 8 negative values, empty cells, 40 demand values and 80 revenue values typed as text, 16 spellings of the four service lines, two date formats, eight spellings of a yes/no campaign flag, and 100 duplicate rows), and saved it as `datasets/veda_demand/raw/demand_raw.csv` (4,404 rows). For the time-series preprocessing, I wrote one function, `clean_demand()`, driven by a short specification, that removes duplicates (exact copies and days exported twice), parses both date formats, standardizes the service line names, converts the numbers typed as text, sets impossible values to missing, rebuilds the complete calendar (one row for every day and line, which makes missing days visible), finds the outage (five or more zero days in a row, because weekends and holidays alone give shorter runs of zeros), finds the spikes (more than four times the typical value of the same weekday in the neighboring weeks, and only when the revenue does not agree with the value), and fills the gaps (from the revenue when it is known, and otherwise with the median of the same weekday in the neighboring weeks, which keeps the weekly pattern), flagging every filled value. It turned 4,404 raw rows into 4,384 clean rows, and all the validation checks pass. Because the data is simulated, I could check the pipeline against the original: 99.98% of the rows that were never damaged are identical, every missing day, outage day, negative value, and empty cell was found, 11 of the 12 spikes were found, and the 157 filled demand values are on average 1.76 units away from the truth, against 2.24 for a plain line average. The first version of the pipeline flagged 26 real zero days (it counted three zeros as an outage) and 12 real high days (it checked the units alone), which is why the final version needs five zero days and a second source (the revenue). I saved the cleaned daily table, a weekly table (156 complete Monday-to-Sunday weeks), a monthly table (36 months), and the holiday calendar in `datasets/veda_demand/`. For the monthly and weekly demand visualization, I plotted the monthly demand by line (adjusted to a 30.4-day month), the weekly demand by line with a 13-week moving average, the demand by weekday relative to an average day, and the shares of demand and of revenue by line, and I repeated the monthly totals in SQL with `sqlite3` to confirm that they match pandas.

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, I had a reproducible simulated demand history, a time-series preprocessing pipeline that restored it, and the first look at the demand that prepares Day 46. The history has 17,564 units of demand and INR 349.0 million of revenue. Total demand grew from 4,938 units in the first year to 5,766 and then 6,860 (+38.9% in two years), and the lines grew at very different speeds: Digital Services +75.6%, Training Programs +49.7%, Internship Programs +26.8%, and IT Services +18.1%. Each line has its own peak month (January for Internship Programs, July for Training Programs, March for IT Services, and July and August for Digital Services), Monday to Thursday are 10% to 35% above an average day while the weekend is far lower (only 9.1% of the IT Services demand falls on weekends), and the change built into the last months is visible: from June 2026 Digital Services run 57.5% above the same months of the year before, while IT Services fall 7.7% below. Demand and revenue are very unequal: Internship Programs are 39% of the units and 2.3% of the revenue, while IT Services are 15.8% of the units and 68.4% of the revenue. Because the data is simulated, these findings reflect the assumptions I built into it, so they practice the analysis workflow and do not describe the real company. The main lesson was that time-series cleaning needs time-series logic: every day must exist, a filled value should respect the weekday pattern, and an outage or a spike has to be judged against its neighbors and against a second source, because the same raw value can be a real event or an error.

## Part 2 - Day 46: Sales & Service Demand Forecasting System (Part 2)

I started from the cleaned daily, weekly, and monthly tables of Day 45. For the trend analysis, I plotted the rolling 52-week total of each line (which removes the yearly pattern, so the line shows only growth) and decomposed the monthly series, on a log scale so that seasonality is a percentage, with STL (trend, season, and remainder). I fitted a straight line to the seasonally adjusted months before June 2026 and extended it, to show what a forecaster who trusted the old trend would expect, and I computed the year-over-year growth, which cancels the season. For the seasonality analysis, I turned the seasonal part of STL into a seasonal index by calendar month (1.00 is an average month) and showed it as a heatmap, measured the strength of the yearly and weekly patterns (1 minus the variance of the remainder over the variance of the seasonal part plus the remainder), and fitted a Poisson regression for each line (a model for counts, with robust standard errors) on the days before June 2026 with the trend, month, weekday, holiday, and campaign together, which separates a campaign in July from a July effect and gives confidence intervals. Because the data is simulated, I could grade all of this against the values used to generate it. The first version of the decomposition used robust STL, and the seasonally adjusted line on the chart was visibly wrong: with only 36 months, the robust version of this statsmodels release returned residuals of exactly zero at both ends of the series, so I switched to the standard version and checked that the residuals were no longer zero at the ends. For the baseline forecasting model, I forecast weekly demand and split the history in time, never at random: the 130 weeks until March 23, 2026 for training and the last 26 weeks (March 30 to September 27, 2026) for testing, which includes 17 weeks after the change in the last months, so the models of Day 47 are judged on the same weeks. I built six baselines using only the training weeks: the naive forecast (the last week), moving averages of 4 and 13 weeks, the seasonal naive forecast (the same week one year earlier), the seasonal naive forecast multiplied by the growth of the last 52 weeks, and the same forecast smoothed over three weeks. I evaluated them with MAE, WAPE (the total error over the total demand), and bias, and avoided MAPE because weekly counts can be small. I saved the seasonal index, the regression effects, the baseline forecasts, and the baseline metrics in `datasets/veda_demand/analysis/` and `forecasts/`.

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, I had a trend analysis, a seasonality analysis, and a set of baseline forecasts with a score for each. Before June 2026, demand grew by about 10% to 25% a year depending on the line, with Digital Services and Training Programs growing fastest and IT Services almost flat since late 2025. The yearly pattern is strong (strength 0.79 to 0.91) and its peaks are right for all four lines, the weekly pattern is weaker (0.25 to 0.37), holidays remove 44% to 55% of the demand, and campaigns add 16% to 35%; all the confidence intervals of the regression contain the true values, and the regression recovered the growth better than the STL line for every line. Among the baselines, the seasonal naive forecast was 15.3% too low in total, multiplying it by the recent growth removed that bias, and smoothing it over three weeks gave the best result, a WAPE of 10.3% for the weekly total, against 12.5% for the 13-week average. The total hides the weakness of every baseline: after June 1, the best baseline is 21.5% too low for Digital Services and 24.3% too high for IT Services, and the two errors cancel in the total. Each line also misses by 15% to 27% per week even in the best case, because weekly counts are noisy. Because the data is simulated, these results show that the methods work, and the best baseline was chosen by its test result, so its score is the benchmark for Day 47, not a promise. The main lesson was to check every chart against the numbers behind it, because a decomposition can fail silently, and to look at the lines separately, because a good total can hide large errors in the parts.

## Part 3 - Day 47: Sales & Service Demand Forecasting System (Part 3)

I used the same split as Day 46 (the 130 weeks until March 23, 2026 for training, and the next 26 weeks for the test), so the new models are scored on the same weeks as the baselines and can be compared with them. Choosing a model by looking at the test would be cheating, so every choice was made on a validation period inside the training data (models fitted on the first 104 weeks and tried on the next 26), and the test was looked at only after the choices were made. I compared two statistical models and one machine-learning model. Holt-Winters (exponential smoothing with a 52-week season) uses only the past of each line. A Poisson regression for each line (a model for counts, in `statsmodels`) models the expected weekly demand as the exponential of a trend, a yearly season described by three pairs of sine and cosine waves (the number of pairs was chosen on the validation), the number of campaign days in the week, and the number of public holidays in the week; it can use the campaign plan and the holiday calendar of the weeks to be forecast, which are known in advance. The machine-learning model is a random forest in `scikit-learn` that forecasts every horizon from 1 to 26 weeks with one model (the direct method): each example is a pair (week the forecast starts, week to forecast), the target is the demand as a multiple of the average of the last 52 weeks (a forest cannot extrapolate a trend, so a relative target solves this), and the features are the horizon, the recent level against the year level, the same week of last year (with its neighbors), the campaign days and holidays, and the season; its depth was chosen on the validation. I scored the models with MAE, WAPE, and bias on each line and on the weekly total, compared them with the best baselines of Day 46, split the error by period (before and after the change in the last months) and by how far ahead the forecast looks, checked what the random forest relies on, and tested how much of the regression's advantage comes from the planned campaigns and holidays by refitting it without them. I also built 80% prediction ranges from the validation errors and checked them on the test weeks. Finally, I refitted the selected model on all 156 weeks and forecast the next 26 weeks (September 28, 2026 to March 22, 2027) with the campaign load of the last year assumed, adding a level correction (the ratio of the actual to the fitted demand over the last 13 weeks), which a backtest at five starting weeks showed to cost half a point of error on average when nothing has changed, and I saved the forecasts with and without the correction. All the results are in `datasets/veda_demand/forecasts/` (the validation scores, the forecasts and scores for the test weeks, and the forecast of the next 26 weeks).

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, I had a forecasting model selected on a validation period and tested on weeks it had never seen. The Poisson regression was the best model: its WAPE on the weekly total of the four lines was 7.2%, against 10.3% for the best baseline of Day 46, 10.4% for the random forest, 11.9% for Holt-Winters, and 12.5% for the 13-week average, with almost no bias (-0.3%). By line, its error was 10.5% for Internship Programs, 19.0% for Training Programs, 23.3% for IT Services, and 20.7% for Digital Services; the regression is off by 14.5% to 20.9% even on the weeks it was fitted on, so the errors of the lines are close to the randomness of the data. Most of the regression's advantage comes from knowing the plan: without the campaign days and holidays, its error on the test total rises from 7.2% to 9.5%, and the advantage was concentrated before June (4.8% against 13.9% to 17.2% for the other methods), because spring 2026 had about half the campaign days of spring 2025 in two lines. From June 1, when Digital Services lifted and IT Services slowed down, all methods were within 8.1% to 9.7% on the total, and every method under-forecast Digital Services; the 80% ranges contained 76.9% of the test line-weeks, but only 52.9% of the Digital Services weeks after June 1. The forecast for the next 26 weeks has 1,410 units for Internship Programs, 979 for Training Programs, 465 for IT Services, and 778 for Digital Services (+5%, +11%, -7%, and +45% against the same weeks a year earlier), with the busiest weeks in January and February and the quietest in December. Because the data is simulated, these results show that the methods work and where they break; they do not describe the real company. The main lesson was that a forecast is only as good as the information given to it: the plan of the weeks to forecast helped more than a more flexible model, and no model could foresee a change that is not in its history.

## Part 4 - Day 48: Sales & Service Demand Forecasting System (Part 4)

I started from the forecast of the next 26 weeks (September 28, 2026 to March 22, 2027) saved on Day 47 and first used the test weeks to explain why historical trends do not always predict future demand: after June 1, 2026 the demand of two lines changed level, and I measured how far the forecast of the selected model was from what happened, plotted the cumulative miss, and built a simple monitor (the demand of the last 8 weeks against the forecast for the same weeks, with a band of +/-20%) to see how long it takes to notice such a change. For planning, I refitted the selected Poisson regression on all 156 weeks exactly as on Day 47 (the same trend, season, and level correction, and I checked that it reproduces the saved forecast) and ran it under three campaign plans (no campaigns, the typical load of the last 52 weeks, and 50% more campaign days), converted units to rupees with the revenue per unit of each line over the last 52 weeks, compared everything with the same 26 weeks one year earlier, and found the busiest weeks with the top of their ranges as the demand to plan capacity for. Months have four or five weeks, so the plan by month is shown in units per week, with each week assigned to the month of its Thursday. From these tables I wrote seven business planning recommendations, each with the evidence calculated from the data, an action, and an impact that is labeled as a scenario with its assumptions, and saved them in `business_planning_recommendations.csv`. The future demand dashboard exists in two forms: a static page (`future_demand_dashboard.png`) with the demand tiles, the weekly forecasts with their ranges, the plan by month, the accuracy of the methods, the value of the campaign plan, and the monitor, and an interactive Streamlit app (`app.py`) with filters for the service lines, the campaign plan, the range, and the alert band, which I tested with Streamlit's test runner and by opening it in a browser. I also wrote a final report (`final_report.md`) that summarizes the four days. A mistake that I found on the way: the monthly table of Day 47 counted weeks in the month of their Monday, which made November look like a peak and December like a dip only because of five and four weeks, so I corrected that table and its observation (the forecast files did not change).

**Key results and findings:**

By the end of this task, I had a complete forecasting system with a dashboard and planning recommendations. The forecast for the next 26 weeks is 3,632 units, 11.7% more than the same weeks a year earlier, but the revenue is only 2.4% higher (INR 65.8 million against INR 64.2 million), because Digital Services are forecast 45% higher (778 units) while IT Services, which are 64% of the revenue, are forecast 7% lower (465 units). Per week, demand is highest in January and February (about 150 units) and lowest in December (about 126). The campaign plan alone moves the 26-week total from 3,375 units (no campaigns) to 3,632 (typical) and 3,768 (50% more campaign days), and it is worth the most money in IT Services (INR 1.4 million for the typical campaigns, with a very uncertain effect) and the least in Internship Programs (INR 0.1 million). The weeks after June 1 showed the limit of history: Digital Services ran 19% above and IT Services 19% below the forecast, and the monitor flagged them only 15 and 4 weeks after the changes began, while it never flagged the two stable lines. Because the data is simulated, these results show the workflow and the pitfalls, and the monitor band was chosen after seeing the lines, so it is an illustration. The main lesson was that the useful output of a forecast is a decision under uncertainty: ranges, scenarios, revenue and not only units, and a weekly check against reality.
